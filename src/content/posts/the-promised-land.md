When I was a kid growing up in Northern Ireland, Croke Park felt like the promised land. It wasn't just another stadium. Croker was different.

In 1986, at least one of my older brothers travelled down for Tyrone's first All-Ireland final against Kerry. My memory insists it was both of them, which tells you something about how the grievance has aged. I was too young to go. Kerry won, but that isn't the bit I remember. I remember being left behind.

Forty years on, it still stings, which is ridiculous really. I've since travelled to hundreds of stadiums, football grounds, athletics tracks, arenas and ballparks around the world. I've watched sport in places the little boy stuck at home in Northern Ireland could never have imagined visiting.

But I didn't go to Croke Park with my brothers. Apparently I'm holding onto that one.

Sport was wrapped up in identity when I was growing up. In Northern Ireland in the 1980s it was difficult for it not to be. And before Katie Taylor, there was Barry McGuigan.

I was very young when McGuigan beat Eusebio Pedroza at Loftus Road in 1985, but he was enormous. You probably have to have lived through that period to understand quite how significant he was. A Catholic from Clones, married to a Protestant woman, fighting through some of the darkest years of the Troubles, he managed something very few people could. People on both sides wanted him to win.

He didn't pretend identity didn't matter. He found a way of carrying it without making it a weapon. For a kid growing up there, that mattered more than I understood at the time.

At eight, I moved to England. I'm 45 now, so almost my entire life has happened here. I went to school here, I've worked here, my friends are here and my children were born here. Liverpool is home.

But I've never stopped being Irish.

It becomes a slightly different kind of Irishness when you live away. The accent goes, your reference points broaden and you can go long periods without consciously thinking about it. Then Ireland score, you hear an Irish crowd somewhere overseas or Katie Taylor walks towards a boxing ring, and there it is again. Fully intact.

That is why the announcement of Katie Taylor's final fight hit me immediately. Not simply because it was the last fight of an extraordinary career, but because it was at Croke Park.

There was never much chance of me going. My eldest was competing for Liverpool Harriers in the Youth Development League national final on the same day and there was no decision to make. I was exactly where I should have been.

There is something rather lovely about that. The child who once desperately wanted to follow his older brothers to a huge sporting occasion was now staying in England to watch his own child compete in one.

Before his race, he even got the chance to have a few words with fellow Liverpool Harrier Katarina Johnson-Thompson. He had his sporting day. I had mine.

Still, I'd be lying if I said there wasn't a little part of me thinking: Katie Taylor. Her last fight. Croke Park. For God's sake.

Eddie Hearn gets plenty of stick, some of it deserved. Boxing promoters are not generally a profession troubled by understatement. But from the moment the fight was announced, Hearn understood that Croke Park was part of the significance, not simply the building big enough to hold the crowd. He sold it as something special because it was something special.

Croke Park is not just an 80,000-seat stadium. For generations of Irish people it has carried sport, history, culture and identity. It is sacred ground. Professional boxing had not been staged there since Muhammad Ali fought Al "Blue" Lewis in 1972. Fifty-four years later, Katie Taylor got the next line in that history. Even a boxing promoter couldn't manufacture the significance of that. He just had to recognise it.

And what a way to finish.

Taylor controlled her final fight from start to finish, won on points and walked away an undisputed champion. Two of the judges gave her every round. There was no desperate final-round rescue and no sense that the occasion had overwhelmed the sport. She simply did what she has spent a career doing.

She was better.

There will rightly be plenty written about the Olympic gold medal, the world titles, Madison Square Garden, Amanda Serrano and everything Taylor has done for women's boxing. But perhaps the simplest way to understand her impact is to look at where she started and where she finished.

She began in a sport where girls struggled simply to get fights. She finished with 80,000 people at Croke Park watching her leave boxing as an undisputed champion.

That is some distance travelled.

There are now girls walking into boxing gyms across Ireland for whom being there is completely unremarkable. Taylor helped make something that once seemed extraordinary feel normal. That may ultimately be a bigger legacy than any belt.

There is an Irishness to Katie Taylor that I find incredibly affecting. It doesn't feel defensive and it doesn't require anybody else to be less Irish, less British or anything else. She carries the flag and represents the country with a confidence and joy that I love.

McGuigan had to carry his identity carefully, because the alternative was dangerous. Taylor gets to carry hers lightly. That she can is partly his generation's doing, and partly hers.

Watching her makes me properly, stupidly proud. Proud of this tiny island that has a remarkable habit of producing people capable of going out into the world and becoming extraordinary, and proud, in a way I probably don't say often enough, of being Irish myself.

Maybe living away sharpens that.

I've spent nearly four decades in England and built my life here. My children are growing up with their own places, their own identities and their own sporting memories. On the day of Katie Taylor's final fight, I was watching my son represent his Liverpool athletics club while, across the Irish Sea, 80,000 people were gathering at the stadium I had mythologised as a child.

I wouldn't have swapped places.

But there are parts of childhood that never completely leave you. Croke Park is one of mine. Barry McGuigan is another. And now Katie Taylor belongs there too. It is not the first time I have found a memory of my brothers [attached to something unlikely](/blog/has-anybody-seen-richie/).

When she walked out for the final time, part of me was still the little boy whose older brothers had disappeared off to Dublin while he stayed at home, with the promised land somewhere just beyond reach.

Watching an Irish woman win there and leave boxing as an undisputed champion, the distance didn't seem quite so great.

I love being Irish.
