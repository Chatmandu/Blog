Third-party course listings have always mattered for student recruitment. AI could make keeping them accurate much more important.

In July, details emerged of an agreement under which Yelp licenses its reviews, ratings, photos and business information to OpenAI, giving ChatGPT access to another source of real-time information when people ask for local recommendations. Yelp first disclosed the deal in its February 2026 results; the scope became clearer in July, when it was reported to cover around 330 million cumulative reviews and more than eight million business listings. At first glance, that has very little to do with higher education marketing, but I think it raises a question we should be paying much more attention to: who describes your university to the machines?

Most of the conversation about AI visibility has focused on what organisations publish themselves. We are told to make our websites easier for AI systems to understand, improve our content structure, strengthen the relationships between pages and provide clear answers to the questions people are likely to ask. All of that matters, but it assumes our own website is the most important version of us. I have made a version of that argument [about individual researchers](/blog/the-researcher-ai-cant-see-and-how-to-fix-it/), where the same problem appears at a much smaller scale.

The Yelp agreement is a useful reminder that AI systems have other sources. OpenAI says its models are developed using information that is publicly available online, information it partners with third parties to access, and information provided by users, trainers and researchers. Its agreement with Reddit, for example, provides access to Reddit's real-time structured content through its Data API.

There is no evidence that OpenAI has an equivalent agreement with the major higher education course aggregators, and I am not suggesting it does. The more interesting point is that an AI system does not have to accept the version of your institution that appears on your website as the definitive one, and universities have spent decades creating alternative versions of themselves.

## We already give our information to other people

Third-party course listings are hardly new. Universities provide course information to directories, aggregators, rankings organisations and other platforms because prospective students use them. They can generate awareness, referral traffic and enquiries, particularly when someone is still comparing institutions rather than searching for a specific university.

FindAMasters, for example, provides both links through to university websites and the ability for prospective students to register their interest directly through its platform. There is already a perfectly good marketing reason to make sure those listings are accurate, and at LSTM improving and maintaining our third-party course information is something we are prioritising as part of our wider postgraduate recruitment work.

AI adds another potential reason to care about it. The audience for a course listing may no longer only be the people who visit that website. It may also form part of the wider information environment from which machines learn about, discover and compare our courses. That gives fairly routine data maintenance a different strategic significance.

## One course, two versions: what the listings actually say

I tested this with something I know well: LSTM's online MSc Global Health.

Our current course page describes a 100% online, part-time master's designed to be flexible around working professionals. Students can complete it over two to five years and the 2026 tuition fee is £14,939 for the whole course for both UK and international students.

FindAMasters currently reflects the two-to-five-year duration and directs students to LSTM for current fee information. Its record was last updated on 6 July 2026. Another major postgraduate platform, Mastersportal, currently describes the same MSc Global Health as lasting 24 months and lists tuition at £14,490 per year.

Someone, or something, trying to understand this fairly simple course can therefore encounter two quite different versions. One says two to five years and £14,939 for the whole course. Another says 24 months and £14,490 per year. That is not an obscure difference buried in the module information. Cost and flexibility are likely to be among the most important factors for someone considering an online master's alongside work.

Nor do I think this is evidence that anyone is doing a particularly bad job. University course information changes constantly. Fees change, courses are redesigned, copy is rewritten and feeds and platforms update at different times. Some services pull information automatically while others depend on supplied data or manual updates. Keeping every representation of an institution perfectly synchronised is difficult.

Universities have lived with that problem for years. What is changing is the potential audience for those representations.

## The intermediary may be more important than its traffic suggests

Traditionally, I might judge a third-party course platform largely by what it sends back to us. How many referrals did it generate? How many enquiries? How many applications can we attribute to it? Those remain sensible questions, but they may no longer tell us the whole story.

Imagine a directory sends relatively little direct traffic to your university website while holding thousands of well-structured course records covering most of UK higher education. Its direct audience might be modest compared with Google, but its value as a dataset could be something else entirely.

That is the lesson I take from Yelp. Yelp has spent years building structured information about millions of businesses. OpenAI does not need to recreate that dataset from scratch if it can establish a relationship with someone who already owns it.

Higher education has its own information intermediaries. Course directories, student review platforms, league tables, government datasets, institutional profiles and other specialist services all hold structured versions of who we are and what we offer. We do not know which of those sources will become particularly important to AI systems, and some may never do so, but it feels increasingly risky to assess their importance purely by how many clicks they send us today.

## What to check in your third-party course listings

- Course title, fee, duration, mode of study and start date
- Whether the link goes to the right place
- Programmes that no longer exist and should be removed
- Whether the thing that makes the course distinctive has survived the journey between your website and somebody else's database

None of that requires a generative AI strategy, but it does require ownership, processes and time. For marketing teams, it also means expanding what we think of as managing our digital estate. Our website is the bit we own, but it is not the only place where our institution exists. The same logic applies at individual level, which is the argument behind my [AI visibility checklist for researchers](/blog/ai-visibility-for-researchers-checklist/).

At LSTM, we are already prioritising the accuracy and quality of our third-party listings because they form part of the prospective student journey. What the changing AI landscape has done is give that work an additional dimension. We are not just maintaining listings for the people who might find them directly. We may also be maintaining some of the source material from which future discovery systems understand us.

## Your website might not get the final word

I do not think the answer is to try to control every description of a university online. That would be impossible and undesirable. Students should have independent sources of information, reviews should reflect what students actually think, rankings should apply their own methodologies and journalists should describe institutions independently.

But factual information is different. Whether a master's costs £14,939 for the entire course or £14,490 every year is not a difference of opinion.

As more prospective students ask AI systems to find courses, shortlist universities and compare their options, those factual discrepancies matter. The challenge is that we cannot see the full information supply chain behind every AI answer. Licensing agreements are announced, but how individual sources influence particular answers is generally not disclosed. We should therefore be cautious about claiming that a particular third-party listing is determining what ChatGPT, Gemini or another assistant says.

We can, however, see the direction in which the information environment is moving. The open web is no longer the only game in town. AI companies can combine publicly available material with search, APIs, structured datasets and information accessed through commercial partnerships. OpenAI explicitly acknowledges third-party partnerships as one of the sources of information used in developing its models.

That changes the question I think university marketers should ask. For years we have worried about whether prospective students can find the right information on our websites. Perhaps we should now ask something broader: if a machine was trying to understand our courses, how many different versions of us would it find, and are we comfortable with what they say?
